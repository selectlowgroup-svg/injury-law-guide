Steps to Take Immediately After a Car Accident in Arizona

Step 1: Check for Injuries and Ensure Safety

Prioritize health and safety above all else. First, check yourself and your passengers for any injuries. If anyone is hurt – even if injuries seem minor – call 911 right away to get medical help on the scene. Describe your location and let the dispatcher know if emergency medical assistance is needed. Do not move anyone who appears seriously injured (especially if they have possible neck or back injuries) unless they are in immediate danger (for example, if there’s a fire). Moving a severely injured person can worsen their injuries, so wait for paramedics unless a life-threatening hazard forces quick action.

While checking for injuries, stay calm and keep yourself safe. If you’re in a position that risks further harm (such as stopped in the middle of a busy road), and you are able to move, take precautions (like turning on hazard lights) to warn other drivers. Arizona law requires drivers involved in a crash to remain at the scene and provide aid or information. Leaving the scene of an accident before exchanging information or talking to police can result in serious hit-and-run charges with severe penalties (including potential felony charges if someone is injured).

Step 2: Move to a Safe Location (If Possible)

If your vehicle is drivable and no one has life-threatening injuries, move your car out of active traffic lanes to a safe spot nearby, such as the shoulder of the road or a parking lot. This helps prevent secondary accidents. Turn on your hazard lights to alert other drivers of the situation. Arizona authorities advise moving vehicles out of travel lanes after minor crashes if you can do so safely.

If a vehicle cannot be moved or if someone is injured and must stay put, do what you can to make the scene safe. Keep your hazard lights on, use road flares or warning triangles if you have them, and stay a safe distance from moving traffic. Stand off to the side or on a sidewalk/shoulder while you wait for police or tow trucks.

Step 3: Call 911 and Report the Accident

Even if it seems minor, calling the police (or at least getting the incident documented) can save you headaches later—especially if the other driver changes their story or you start feeling pain a day or two after the crash. In Arizona, you are legally required to report an accident to law enforcement if anyone is injure. Even when it’s ‘just’ property damage, calling 911 is usually smart because it creates an official record that can help with insurance later. Given modern vehicle repair costs, even what looks like a moderate fender-bender can easily exceed this threshold. When in doubt, call 911 – this ensures an official accident report is created, which can be critical evidence for insurance claims or legal cases later.

When you speak to the 911 dispatcher, remain calm and provide clear information about the location of the accident and describe any known injuries. If the police arrive, cooperate fully and stick to the facts when describing what happened. Do not admit fault or blame anyone at the scene – simply recount the events objectively. The officer will compile the details into an accident report.

Tip: Make a note of the police report number if an officer creates one. This number will allow you to obtain the full report later and will be requested by insurance companies.

Step 4: Exchange Information with Other Parties

After ensuring everyone’s immediate safety and calling for help, exchange information with the other driver(s) involved. Arizona law requires drivers in an accident to provide certain information to each other. You should exchange the following details:

  • Name and Contact Information: Full name, phone number, and home address.
  • Driver’s License: Show your driver’s license and note the other driver’s license number.
  • Vehicle Information: Make, model, year, color of the vehicle, and license plate number.
  • Insurance Details: The name of their auto insurance company and the policy number.

Additionally, ask for contact information of any independent witnesses who saw the crash happen. Bystanders or other drivers who witnessed the accident can provide unbiased statements later, which can be invaluable if there is a dispute about what occurred.

When talking with the other driver(s), keep the conversation courteous and strictly factual. Do not apologize or say “It was my fault” – even a polite “I’m sorry” could be construed as admitting fault. Similarly, avoid getting angry or accusing the other driver. Stick to exchanging the necessary information and make sure everyone gets the details they need.

Step 5: Document the Accident Scene

While still at the scene, if it’s safe to do so, gather as much evidence as possible. Your smartphone is your best tool here. Take photos and videos from multiple angles to thoroughly document the following:

  • Vehicle Damage: Photograph all visible damage on your car and the other vehicle(s). Get close-up shots of dents, scratches, broken glass, deployed airbags, etc., as well as wider shots showing each entire vehicle.
  • The Overall Scene: Capture the positions of the vehicles relative to the road. Include any skid marks on the pavement, debris, or vehicle parts on the ground, and the surrounding area (intersections, traffic signals, stop signs, landmarks).
  • Road & Weather Conditions: Take pictures of anything relevant, such as wet roads, ice, obscured traffic signs, or poor lighting.
  • License Plates and Documents: Photograph the other car’s license plate and any documents the other driver shows you.

It’s also helpful to jot down your own recollection of how the accident occurred while it’s fresh in your mind. Note the time and date, the direction each vehicle was traveling, and anything you observed. The goal of documenting the scene is to preserve evidence. Memories can fade and stories might change, but photos and contemporaneous notes provide hard evidence.

Step 6: Notify Your Insurance Company

After you’ve left the scene and addressed any immediate injuries, you’ll need to report the accident to your insurance company as soon as reasonably possible. Most insurance policies require prompt notification of any collisions. Ideally, notify your insurer within 24 to 48 hours of the accident.

When you speak with your insurance company, stick to the facts of what happened. Provide the date, time, and location of the crash, the identities of the other parties, and a basic description of the incident. Avoid making statements that speculate about fault or cause. Similarly, don’t downplay your injuries; if you have any pain or discomfort, mention it.

It’s often best not to give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurance company until you’ve consulted with an attorney. Never sign any settlement agreement or release of claims without careful consideration. Insurance companies sometimes offer quick, low-ball settlements to close the case early, but accepting a fast check could forfeit your right to full compensation.

Keep in mind that Arizona is an “at-fault” state for auto insurance, meaning the insurance of the driver who caused the accident is responsible for covering damages. By notifying your insurer and cooperating with the claims process, you help ensure that your coverage will be available when you need it.

Step 7: Seek Medical Attention (Even If You Feel Fine)

Some injuries from car accidents are immediately obvious – but many injuries only become apparent hours or days later due to adrenaline and shock masking the pain. This is why it’s critical to get a medical evaluation as soon as possible after the accident, even if you feel “okay” at first. Injuries like whiplash, concussions, internal bleeding, or soft tissue strains might not show symptoms right away.

Common delayed symptoms to watch for include neck or shoulder pain, headaches or dizziness, abdominal pain, or numbness/tingling. If you notice any of these in the hours or days after the crash, seek medical care immediately. Consider seeing a doctor within 24-72 hours of the accident as a precaution.

After the initial check-up, follow all medical advice and treatment plans diligently. Keep copies of all medical records, bills, and discharge papers. Not only does this help you recover fully, but it also provides a clear record linking your injuries to the accident – which will be vital if you pursue compensation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I need to call the police for a minor fender-bender in Arizona? A: AArizona law requires you to notify law enforcement if a crash results in injury or death. While police are not legally required to respond to every minor property-damage-only accident, it’s still a good idea to call or file a report whenever possible. An official report can be very helpful later if injuries appear or there is a dispute with insurance. Never leave the scene without exchanging required information.

Q: Should I see a doctor even if I feel fine after the accident? A: Yes, absolutely. After an accident your adrenaline is high, which can mask pain and injury symptoms. Injuries like whiplash, soft tissue strains, concussions, or internal injuries may not show obvious signs right away. Medical professionals advise getting a check-up as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours of the accident.

Q: Can I still recover damages if I was partially at fault for the car accident? A: Yes. Arizona follows a “pure comparative negligence” rule. This means each party can still recover compensation even if they were partly to blame, but their recovery is reduced by their percentage of fault. For example, if you were 20% at fault and the other driver 80% at fault, you could recover 80% of your damages.

A: In Arizona, the statute of limitations for filing a personal injury lawsuit from a car accident is generally two years from the date of the accident. Is Oregon a no-fault state?

No. Oregon is a fault state: the driver who caused the crash is responsible for your losses through that driver's liability insurance. Oregon also requires PIP on private passenger auto policies, so covered medical bills and lost income are paid whoever caused the crash.

A claim against the driver at fault includes economic losses and pain and suffering, subject to the rules below. The PIP insurer must still pay benefits when you also have a claim against the at-fault driver (ORS 742.520(5)).

What does PIP pay in Oregon?

Oregon PIP pays up to $15,000 per person for reasonable and necessary medical expenses incurred within two years of injury. PIP also pays limited lost income, help at home, funeral expenses, and child care.

The required coverage protects the policyholder, family members in the household, children living there and being raised as the policyholder's own, passengers in the insured car, and pedestrians the car hits. Depending on how you were injured, contact your own insurer, the insurer of the car you occupied, or the insurer of the car that hit you while you were walking. The insurer must pay promptly after you submit proof of loss (ORS 742.520).

The 60-day medical-bill rule

Medical charges are presumed reasonable and necessary unless the provider receives a denial within 60 calendar days after the insurer receives the provider's claim. Within those 60 days, the insurer must also give you a written denial explaining the reason and how to contest it (ORS 742.524 and 742.528).

The Oregon Division of Financial Regulation says health insurance will usually wait to cover crash-related medical expenses until your PIP benefits have been used. The division describes PIP medical coverage as reasonable and necessary expenses within two years, up to $15,000 or your PIP limit.

Example: lost income above the monthly limit

For example, 70% of a $5,000 monthly income is $3,500. PIP pays the $3,000 monthly maximum, for up to 52 weeks, when disability lasts at least 14 days. Payment ends sooner when the worker can return to the usual job.

Do you have to pay PIP back after a settlement?

You do not have to repay PIP from every settlement. For policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2020, repayment through a lien or subrogation comes only from settlement money left after you receive full compensation. You never repay more than the PIP benefits you received.

Repayment routeWhere the money comes from
Between insurers The PIP insurer asks the at-fault driver's insurer for repayment, reduced by your share of fault. That repayment and the liability insurer's payment to injured people together must fit within the liability policy limit. You do not make the repayment yourself (ORS 742.534).
A lien on your recovery A lien is a claim to part of the money you recover. The PIP insurer chooses this route by written notice within 30 days after learning of your claim or lawsuit. Can you sue for pain and suffering in Oregon?

Yes. Since January 1, 2022, the $500,000 cap in ORS 31.710 has covered only death claims. The cap excludes injury claims where the person lives, and public body claims have their own caps.

Pain and suffering is part of what Oregon calls "noneconomic damages," meaning personal losses such as pain and emotional distress. The law includes "pain, mental suffering, emotional distress, humiliation, injury to reputation, loss of care, comfort, companionship and society, loss of consortium, inconvenience and interference with normal and usual activities apart from gainful employment" (ORS 31.705).

Economic damages cover financial losses. The statute includes "reasonable charges necessarily incurred for medical, hospital, nursing and rehabilitative services and other health care services, burial and memorial expenses, loss of income and past and future impairment of earning capacity" (ORS 31.705).

A jury decides the amount for pain and suffering, and the jury is not told of the death cap. The pain and suffering calculator explains the ways insurers and lawyers value these losses.

What changed in 2022?

The legislature changed the cap from a limit covering bodily injury, death, and property damage to one for wrongful death. The change came through Senate Bill 193, Oregon Laws 2021, chapter 478, approved July 14, 2021 and effective January 1, 2022. The change applies to claims arising before, on, or after that date, except awards with a final judgment entered before the effective date.

Before that change, a pedestrian's leg was amputated after a garbage truck hit him in downtown Portland. The Oregon Supreme Court's July 9, 2020 ruling in Busch v. McInnis Waste Systems, Inc. preserved the jury's $3,021,922 economic award and $10,500,000 noneconomic award by refusing to apply the cap. The court held that cutting his noneconomic award to $500,000 violated the Oregon Constitution's remedy clause. That ruling addressed the cap as applied to his injury.

Two death-cap rulings in 2026

The Oregon Court of Appeals upheld the reduction of a $2,108,412 noneconomic jury award to $500,000 in Estate of James Ritchie v. Helbig on February 11, 2026. The case concerned a motorcyclist killed in Portland. The Oregon Supreme Court denied review on May 21, 2026, leaving that ruling in place.

The Oregon Court of Appeals decided Estate of Grant Raymond Fisher v. Lee on July 1, 2026. A driver under the influence of narcotics rear-ended a 23-year-old's truck on Highway 26, killing him. The court described the other driver's speed as about 96 miles per hour. The trial court awarded $20 million in noneconomic damages, with $15 million for the family's lost society and companionship and $5 million for his conscious pain and suffering. The trial court then cut the award to $500,000.

The Fisher court rejected the argument that the cap is unconstitutional in all death cases, but held that applying it to this award left the family without a substantial remedy. The Court of Appeals reversed and sent the case back. The appeal proceeded without the defendant taking part, and no Oregon Supreme Court action was found as of November 5, 2026; the result can still change. The death cap remains the rule, with Fisher preventing the reduction of that particular award.

What if you were driving uninsured or impaired in Oregon?

An injured driver who was driving uninsured or under the influence cannot recover pain and suffering in Oregon unless an exception applies. Medical bills, lost income, and other economic losses remain recoverable (ORS 31.715).

The rule concerns the injured person's own driving: the statute applies when the plaintiff was "in violation of ORS 806.010 or 813.010" at the time of the act causing injury. The law provides these exceptions:

  • The other driver was also uninsured or driving under the influence.
  • The injury resulted from an intentional wrongful act.
  • The other driver's conduct amounted to reckless driving or a felony.
  • For the uninsured-driver bar, you had liability coverage within 180 days before the injury and had no uninsured driving during the year immediately before that coverage lapsed.

Example: an uninsured driver's injury claim

For example, an uninsured driver has $20,000 in medical bills and lost income and $40,000 in pain and suffering. Without an exception, the driver can recover the $20,000 in economic losses and loses the $40,000 for pain and suffering. Coverage within the previous 180 days avoids the uninsured-driver bar if the driver also had no uninsured driving in the year before the policy lapsed.

Sources: ORS chapter 31; ORS chapter 806

How does fault change an Oregon settlement?

You can recover compensation in Oregon when your fault is 50% or less, but the amount falls by your percentage of fault. At 51% or more, you recover nothing. Your fault is compared with the combined fault of the other responsible people, including someone you already settled with (ORS 31.600).

What if the other driver has only the minimum insurance in Oregon?

Your own underinsured motorist coverage can pay additional damages after the other driver's $25,000 minimum liability coverage. For policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2016, Oregon stopped subtracting the liability payment from your underinsured coverage limit.

Oregon auto liability policies must include uninsured motorist coverage, which also includes underinsured motorist coverage. The limits match your bodily injury liability limits unless a named insured chooses lower limits in writing. The chosen limits must still meet Oregon's minimum of $25,000 per person and $50,000 per crash (ORS 742.502).

Example: your coverage adds to the other driver's payment

The Oregon Division of Financial Regulation explains the 2016 change with Joe and Jane, each carrying $25,000 limits. Joe's insurer pays his $25,000 liability limit. Before the change, Jane's equal uninsured motorist limit prevented an additional payment; the division says that now "she may be paid up to an additional $25,000" by her own insurer.

For example, you have $100,000 in damages, no PIP payment, and $50,000 of underinsured motorist coverage. The other driver's insurer pays its $25,000 limit, leaving $75,000 unpaid. Your underinsured coverage pays up to its $50,000 limit.

When your insurer has already paid PIP for the same crash, that payment reduces the damages you can recover from it under uninsured or underinsured coverage. The PIP payment leaves your coverage limit unchanged (ORS 742.542).

Take a required policy step within two years

Within two years of the crash, you and the insurer must agree on the amount, either side must start arbitration, you must sue the insurer, or you must sue the uninsured driver. If you sue the driver, you then have two years after that settlement or judgment to start arbitration or sue your insurer (ORS 742.504(12)(a)). Can you get attorney fees in a small Oregon car accident claim?

A successful Oregon claim seeking $10,000 or less can include court-awarded attorney fees if you send the required written demand at least 30 days before suing. A separate rule covers attorney fees in a lawsuit against your own insurer.

The written demand for a claim of $10,000 or less

Send the demand to the defendant and the defendant's insurer, if you know it. Include medical records and bills that reasonably explain the nature and extent of the injury. If you win, the court awards reasonable attorney fees at trial and on appeal, unless the defendant offered at least as much before the lawsuit as the damages awarded. Missing medical records and bills defeats the fee request (ORS 20.080). The car accident demand letter guide covers the written demand.

Example: an offer that included a PIP lien

The Oregon Court of Appeals decided Trapp v. Hodges on August 14, 2024. After a 2019 car crash, the other driver's insurer offered $3,900 "inclusive of all liens." The court read that offer as $1,215.64 for the injured person after the $2,684.36 PIP lien. The jury awarded $2,500, with $2,000 for personal injuries and $500 for economic damages, exceeding that net offer. Attorney fees were available, so the court reversed the fee ruling and sent the case back. The later outcome is unknown.

A lawsuit against your own insurer

When your insurer has not settled within six months after you file proof of loss, a successful policy lawsuit includes reasonable attorney fees if your recovery exceeds the insurer's offer. For PIP and uninsured or underinsured motorist claims, an exception applies if the insurer writes within those six months accepting coverage, narrowing the dispute to the allowed issues, and agreeing to binding arbitration. For PIP, the dispute must concern the amount; for uninsured or underinsured claims, the allowed issues are the other driver's fault and the damages (ORS 742.061).

The Oregon Court of Appeals applied that exception in Doss v. Farmers Insurance Co. on June 24, 2026. The insurer sent the required letter but later denied coverage issues in its court answer. The court held that this denial lost the protection from attorney fees, reversed, and sent the case back. The later outcome is unknown.

Court arbitration for claims of $50,000 or less

Oregon circuit courts require arbitration first for matters involving $50,000 or less. A party seeking a new trial must file within 20 days after the arbitration decision and award are filed and pay a $159 deposit (ORS 36.400 and 36.425).

A party that requests the new trial and does no better can owe money toward the other side's later fees and costs. The amount is 20% of the judgment when the defendant requested the trial, or 10% of the amount claimed in the complaint when the plaintiff requested it (ORS 36.425(5)).

What the claim-handling law requires

An insurer must investigate a claim reasonably, try in good faith to settle promptly and fairly when responsibility is reasonably clear, and avoid forcing a lawsuit through an offer substantially below the amount later recovered. Oregon's unfair claim settlement practices law names these prohibited acts (ORS 746.230(1)):

  • "Refusing to pay claims without conducting a reasonable investigation based on all available information;"
  • "Not attempting, in good faith, to promptly and equitably settle claims in which liability has become reasonably clear;"
  • "Compelling claimants to initiate litigation to recover amounts due by offering substantially less than amounts ultimately recovered in actions brought by such claimants;"

A liability insurer owes its insured driver reasonable care in settling a claim within the policy limit. In the 1992 Georgetown Realty decision, the Oregon Supreme Court recognized the insured's right to sue for negligence when the insurer fails in that duty and a judgment exceeds the limit. That claim belongs to the insured driver.

The Oregon Division of Financial Regulation's accident page says an insurer has up to 45 days to investigate responsibility for the crash. Keep that period in mind when negotiating with the insurance adjuster.

Which medical charges count in an Oregon injury claim?

You can claim the reasonable charges for necessary medical treatment, including charges above what Medicare paid. The Oregon Supreme Court applied that rule in White v. Jubitz Corp. on October 15, 2009.

White involved a collapsed bar stool, not a car crash. The Oregon Supreme Court's 2009 White opinion records $38,977 in bills and $13,400 paid by Medicare; the court rejected reducing the medical award to the Medicare payment. Providers wrote off the rest of the bills. The defendant agreed that the bills were reasonable. The ruling concerns reasonable bills, rather than making every medical charge reasonable.

After a verdict, a court may deduct certain payments from other sources. Oregon protects benefits you must repay, insurance you or your family paid for, life insurance and death benefits, retirement and pension benefits, disability plan benefits, and federal Social Security benefits from that deduction (ORS 31.580).

Does Oregon have a cap on punitive damages?

Oregon's punitive-damages statutes, ORS 31.730 and 31.735, set no dollar limit on a punitive award. Punitive damages require clear and convincing proof of malice or reckless and outrageous indifference to a highly unreasonable risk of harm, with conscious indifference to other people's health, safety, and welfare.

Your first complaint cannot request punitive damages. After filing, you may move the court for permission to add that request (ORS 31.725). The $500,000 death cap excludes punitive damages, and the Oregon Tort Claims Act bars punitive damages against public bodies.

Is a car accident settlement taxable in Oregon?

Compensation received for physical injuries is excluded from federal income under 26 U.S.C. 104(a)(2). Oregon's income tax law starts from federal taxable income under ORS 316.048, carrying that exclusion into Oregon income.

Punitive damages are not excluded. The federal statute also says emotional distress by itself is not a physical injury or physical sickness.

Do you have to report a crash to the Oregon DMV?

Yes. An injury or death requires drivers to submit the Oregon Traffic Collision and Insurance Report to the Oregon DMV within 72 hours. You must file even when police filed a report, and the DMV says to submit it as soon as possible if you missed the deadline.

The DMV also requires a report when damage to your vehicle exceeds $2,500, when damage to any vehicle exceeds $2,500 and any vehicle is towed from the scene, or when damage to property other than a vehicle involved in the crash exceeds $2,500 (ORS 811.720).

Submit the report through DMV2U using the DMV collision-reporting page, mail it to DMV Crash Reporting Unit, 1905 Lana Ave NE, Salem, OR 97314, or fax it to 503-945-5267. Call 503-945-5000 to request the form.

Failing to file leads the DMV to issue a suspension notice. Failure to report is also a Class B traffic violation (ORS 811.725). Submit one report per collision; the DMV says each additional report appears as another collision on your record.

The DMV says it cannot give you a copy of your own report under ORS 802.220(5). Getting a copy of the police crash report is a separate task.

What to do after an Oregon car accident

File the DMV report within 72 hours and open the PIP claim with the insurer covering you. Give earlier notice if a public body was involved, then keep the demand and lawsuit deadlines with your claim records.

1

File the DMV report within 72 hours

Send the Oregon Traffic Collision and Insurance Report to DMV after a crash involving injury or death, damage over $2,500 to your vehicle, or damage over $2,500 to property other than a vehicle involved in the crash. The DMV also requires the report when any vehicle has more than $2,500 in damage and any vehicle is towed from the scene. File your report even when police made a report. If you missed 72 hours, submit it as soon as possible.

2

Open the personal injury protection claim

Contact your own auto insurer, the insurer of the car you were riding in, or the insurer of the car that hit you while you were walking. Submit proof of your medical expenses and other covered losses.

3

Give notice when a public body was involved

For an injury involving a government or transit vehicle or worker, give the public body notice within 180 days. The death-claim notice period is one year. The lawsuit deadline is two years after the loss or injury.

4

Notify the insurer that paid your benefits

Tell any PIP or health insurer that paid your bills about your claim or lawsuit. Give the notice by personal service or registered or certified mail.

5

Send the written demand with your medical bills

For a claim of $10,000 or less, send the defendant and any insurer you know about a written demand at least 30 days before suing. Include medical records and bills that explain the injury and treatment so you preserve the right to ask for attorney fees.

6

Keep the two-year injury filing deadline

File the injury lawsuit within two years. For uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage, also take one of the required policy steps within two years of the crash: agree on the amount, start arbitration, sue your insurer, or sue the uninsured driver.

Oregon car accident settlement questions

What is the average car accident settlement in Oregon?

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) reports an average Oregon bodily injury claim cost of $22,875 in 2022, including insurers' claim-handling costs. That figure measures insurers' costs, not the money an injured person receives. National injury ranges for an Oregon claim start at $5,000 for a sprain and reach $5,000,000+ for a severe brain injury, with treatment, fault, and policy limits affecting the amount.

Is Oregon a no-fault state?

No. Oregon is a fault state: the driver responsible for the crash owes compensation through that driver's liability coverage. Oregon also requires personal injury protection, which pays covered medical bills and lost income whoever caused the crash. You can still claim your losses against the driver at fault.

Do you have to pay PIP back after settlement?

You do not have to repay PIP from every settlement, and repayment cannot exceed the benefits you received. For policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2020, a lien or subrogation claim takes repayment only from money above full compensation, with the insurer sharing the recovery costs and attorney fees. A recovery equal to all available coverage is treated as less than full compensation; a recovery below that coverage is treated as full compensation. Repayment between insurers follows a separate route and counts against the at-fault policy limit. You do not make that payment yourself.

Is PIP coverage per person or per accident?

Oregon's minimum PIP medical benefit is $15,000 per person, for reasonable and necessary expenses incurred within two years of injury. The DMV lists the minimum per person. Lost income and the other PIP benefits have separate limits.

Can you sue for pain and suffering in Oregon?

Yes. Oregon allows pain and suffering claims after a crash, but an uninsured or impaired injured driver faces a separate bar with exceptions. Since January 1, 2022, the $500,000 cap in ORS 31.710 has applied only to death claims, excluding injury claims where the person lives. Public body claims have their own caps. The Oregon Court of Appeals upheld the death cap in Ritchie in 2026, and the Oregon Supreme Court denied review on May 21, 2026. In Fisher on July 1, 2026, the same appeals court ruled against cutting that case's $20 million award to $500,000; the result can still change.

How is pain and suffering calculated in Oregon?

A jury decides compensation for the pain, mental suffering, emotional distress, inconvenience, and disruption of ordinary activities that Oregon law counts as noneconomic damages. The jury is not told about the $500,000 death-claim cap. Your medical bills and lost earnings are separate economic losses.

What is the "no pay, no play" law in Oregon?

An injured driver who was uninsured or driving under the influence loses the right to pain and suffering, while medical bills and lost income remain recoverable. Exceptions cover another driver who was uninsured or impaired, an intentional injury, reckless driving, or conduct that would be a felony. The uninsured-driver bar also has an exception for coverage within the prior 180 days, provided the injured driver had no uninsured driving during the year before coverage lapsed.

What if the other driver has only $25,000 of insurance?

The other driver's minimum policy pays at most $25,000 for your injury claim. Your own underinsured motorist coverage can pay additional damages up to its limit. For policies issued or renewed from January 1, 2016, Oregon stopped subtracting the other driver's liability payment from the underinsured coverage limit. PIP payments reduce the remaining damages owed by your own insurer, while the coverage limit stays the same.

How long after a car accident can you file a claim in Oregon?

You have two years to file an Oregon injury lawsuit. An estate's personal representative has three years for a death claim, starting when the injury causing death was discovered or reasonably should have been discovered, and no later than three years after death. A public body claim needs earlier notice, and uninsured or underinsured coverage requires a listed policy step within two years of the crash.

What if an Oregon city bus or government vehicle caused the crash?

Give the public body notice within 180 days for an injury or one year for a death, and file the lawsuit within two years after the loss or injury. For crashes from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027, the Oregon Judicial Department lists a $902,700 limit for one claimant against a local public body and $2,708,100 against the state. The limits for all claimants are $1,805,300 against a local public body and $5,416,200 against the state.

How long does a car accident settlement take in Oregon?

The Oregon Judicial Department reports that, across all general civil case types, 81.4% of Oregon cases closed in 2025 ended within 180 days and 93.0% within 365 days. The Oregon Judicial Department's 2025 trial report gives 810.67 days as the average filing-to-trial time for 139 civil jury trials, more than two years. The Oregon Division of Financial Regulation's accident page says an insurer has up to 45 days to investigate responsibility for a crash; that is an investigation period, not a settlement date.

Is my settlement taxable in Oregon?

Compensation for physical injuries is excluded from federal income under 26 U.S.C. 104(a)(2), and Oregon's income tax law starts from federal taxable income under ORS 316.048. Punitive damages are not excluded. Emotional distress by itself is not a physical injury under the federal statute.

Can I settle without a lawyer in Oregon?

Yes. When reviewing an Oregon settlement yourself, account for PIP repayment and the amount left after liens and recovery costs. A claim of $10,000 or less also has a rule allowing attorney fees after a proper written demand and a court result that meets the fee rule; a separate fee rule covers suits against your own insurer. What happens if you don't file an accident report in Oregon?

The Oregon DMV must send a suspension notice if you fail to file a required Oregon Traffic Collision and Insurance Report. The report is due within 72 hours if the crash causes injury or death, damage over $2,500 to your vehicle, or damage over $2,500 to property other than a vehicle involved in the crash. The DMV also requires the report when any vehicle has over $2,500 in damage and any vehicle is towed from the scene. File the DMV report even if police made their own report. If you missed that time, the DMV says to file as soon as possible. Failure to report is also a Class B traffic violation.

What is the Time Limit for Neck and Back Injury Settlement Claims?

In West Virginia, you generally have two years from the date of an accident to file a personal injury or property damage lawsuit.

If you miss this deadline, you may lose the right to pursue compensation.

For insurance claims, you should report the accident as soon as possible. Many insurance companies have strict timelines, sometimes within a few days or weeks. Exceptions exist, such as for minors or cases where injuries aren’t immediately apparent.

If you’re outside West Virginia, you may also be wondering how long after an accident can you make a claim. The time limit to file a claim depends on your state’s statute of limitations.

Most states allow between one and six years for personal injury or property damage claims.

Insurance companies often require claims to be filed within days or weeks, so it’s best to report the accident as soon as possible.

Some exceptions may extend the deadline, such as cases involving minors, delayed injuries, or government entities, which often have much shorter filing windows.

How to Secure the Maximum Settlement for a Neck and Back Injury

Getting a fair settlement for a neck and back injury after an accident requires strong evidence, strategic negotiation, and legal expertise. Follow these steps to maximize your compensation:

  • Don’t take the first offer. Insurers count on you settling before you know how hurt you are. [Example: a client from Charleston was rear-ended at a red light; the insurer offered $800 right after the claim. He saw a doctor first, found a herniated disc, and about nine months later settled for over $45,000.]
  • See a doctor and follow the treatment plan. Your medical records are the foundation of the claim, and many neck and back injuries are soft-tissue — real pain that doesn’t show on an X-ray. [Example: a woman from Morgantown sprained her back in a T-bone crash, went to the ER, then skipped her follow-ups; the insurer used the gap to argue she wasn’t badly hurt and offered far less.]
  • Document your pain and how it affects your life. In West Virginia you can recover for pain and suffering, not just bills — so keep a simple daily journal and photos. [Example: a client from Fairmont kept a daily log, including photos on the days she couldn’t get out of bed; the adjuster said it made clear what she was going through, and the settlement came in higher.]
  • Be careful what you say to adjusters. You don’t have to give a recorded statement, and a polite “I’m fine” can be used against you later. [Example: a man from Martinsburg told the adjuster he was “fine, just a little sore”; months later he needed therapy and missed work, and the insurer threw his words back at him. He still won, but it made the case harder.]
  • Don’t settle too soon. Neck and back injuries can take weeks or months to fully show, and once you sign, the case is over — even if you need surgery later. [Example: a young dad from Parkersburg was offered $5,000 right away; his pain worsened into two rounds of physical therapy and weeks off work. He held out and recovered more than $50,000.]
  • Work with the right West Virginia lawyer. A local attorney knows what these injuries are worth in West Virginia courts and works on contingency — no fee unless you win.
