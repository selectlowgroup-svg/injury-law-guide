Steps to Take Immediately After a Car Accident in Arizona

Step 1: Check for Injuries and Ensure Safety

Prioritize health and safety above all else. First, check yourself and your passengers for any injuries. If anyone is hurt – even if injuries seem minor – call 911 right away to get medical help on the scene. Describe your location and let the dispatcher know if emergency medical assistance is needed. Do not move anyone who appears seriously injured (especially if they have possible neck or back injuries) unless they are in immediate danger (for example, if there’s a fire). Moving a severely injured person can worsen their injuries, so wait for paramedics unless a life-threatening hazard forces quick action.

While checking for injuries, stay calm and keep yourself safe. If you’re in a position that risks further harm (such as stopped in the middle of a busy road), and you are able to move, take precautions (like turning on hazard lights) to warn other drivers. Arizona law requires drivers involved in a crash to remain at the scene and provide aid or information. Leaving the scene of an accident before exchanging information or talking to police can result in serious hit-and-run charges with severe penalties (including potential felony charges if someone is injured).

Step 2: Move to a Safe Location (If Possible)

If your vehicle is drivable and no one has life-threatening injuries, move your car out of active traffic lanes to a safe spot nearby, such as the shoulder of the road or a parking lot. This helps prevent secondary accidents. Turn on your hazard lights to alert other drivers of the situation. Arizona authorities advise moving vehicles out of travel lanes after minor crashes if you can do so safely.

If a vehicle cannot be moved or if someone is injured and must stay put, do what you can to make the scene safe. Keep your hazard lights on, use road flares or warning triangles if you have them, and stay a safe distance from moving traffic. Stand off to the side or on a sidewalk/shoulder while you wait for police or tow trucks.

Step 3: Call 911 and Report the Accident

Even if it seems minor, calling the police (or at least getting the incident documented) can save you headaches later—especially if the other driver changes their story or you start feeling pain a day or two after the crash. In Arizona, you are legally required to report an accident to law enforcement if anyone is injure. Even when it’s ‘just’ property damage, calling 911 is usually smart because it creates an official record that can help with insurance later. Given modern vehicle repair costs, even what looks like a moderate fender-bender can easily exceed this threshold. When in doubt, call 911 – this ensures an official accident report is created, which can be critical evidence for insurance claims or legal cases later.

When you speak to the 911 dispatcher, remain calm and provide clear information about the location of the accident and describe any known injuries. If the police arrive, cooperate fully and stick to the facts when describing what happened. Do not admit fault or blame anyone at the scene – simply recount the events objectively. The officer will compile the details into an accident report.

Tip: Make a note of the police report number if an officer creates one. This number will allow you to obtain the full report later and will be requested by insurance companies.

Step 4: Exchange Information with Other Parties

After ensuring everyone’s immediate safety and calling for help, exchange information with the other driver(s) involved. Arizona law requires drivers in an accident to provide certain information to each other. You should exchange the following details:

  • Name and Contact Information: Full name, phone number, and home address.
  • Driver’s License: Show your driver’s license and note the other driver’s license number.
  • Vehicle Information: Make, model, year, color of the vehicle, and license plate number.
  • Insurance Details: The name of their auto insurance company and the policy number.

Additionally, ask for contact information of any independent witnesses who saw the crash happen. Bystanders or other drivers who witnessed the accident can provide unbiased statements later, which can be invaluable if there is a dispute about what occurred.

When talking with the other driver(s), keep the conversation courteous and strictly factual. Do not apologize or say “It was my fault” – even a polite “I’m sorry” could be construed as admitting fault. Similarly, avoid getting angry or accusing the other driver. Stick to exchanging the necessary information and make sure everyone gets the details they need.

Step 5: Document the Accident Scene

While still at the scene, if it’s safe to do so, gather as much evidence as possible. Your smartphone is your best tool here. Take photos and videos from multiple angles to thoroughly document the following:

  • Vehicle Damage: Photograph all visible damage on your car and the other vehicle(s). Get close-up shots of dents, scratches, broken glass, deployed airbags, etc., as well as wider shots showing each entire vehicle.
  • The Overall Scene: Capture the positions of the vehicles relative to the road. Include any skid marks on the pavement, debris, or vehicle parts on the ground, and the surrounding area (intersections, traffic signals, stop signs, landmarks).
  • Road & Weather Conditions: Take pictures of anything relevant, such as wet roads, ice, obscured traffic signs, or poor lighting.
  • License Plates and Documents: Photograph the other car’s license plate and any documents the other driver shows you.

It’s also helpful to jot down your own recollection of how the accident occurred while it’s fresh in your mind. Note the time and date, the direction each vehicle was traveling, and anything you observed. The goal of documenting the scene is to preserve evidence. Memories can fade and stories might change, but photos and contemporaneous notes provide hard evidence.

Step 6: Notify Your Insurance Company

After you’ve left the scene and addressed any immediate injuries, you’ll need to report the accident to your insurance company as soon as reasonably possible. Most insurance policies require prompt notification of any collisions. Ideally, notify your insurer within 24 to 48 hours of the accident.

When you speak with your insurance company, stick to the facts of what happened. Provide the date, time, and location of the crash, the identities of the other parties, and a basic description of the incident. Avoid making statements that speculate about fault or cause. Similarly, don’t downplay your injuries; if you have any pain or discomfort, mention it.

It’s often best not to give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurance company until you’ve consulted with an attorney. Never sign any settlement agreement or release of claims without careful consideration. Insurance companies sometimes offer quick, low-ball settlements to close the case early, but accepting a fast check could forfeit your right to full compensation.

Keep in mind that Arizona is an “at-fault” state for auto insurance, meaning the insurance of the driver who caused the accident is responsible for covering damages. By notifying your insurer and cooperating with the claims process, you help ensure that your coverage will be available when you need it.

Step 7: Seek Medical Attention (Even If You Feel Fine)

Some injuries from car accidents are immediately obvious – but many injuries only become apparent hours or days later due to adrenaline and shock masking the pain. This is why it’s critical to get a medical evaluation as soon as possible after the accident, even if you feel “okay” at first. Injuries like whiplash, concussions, internal bleeding, or soft tissue strains might not show symptoms right away.

Common delayed symptoms to watch for include neck or shoulder pain, headaches or dizziness, abdominal pain, or numbness/tingling. If you notice any of these in the hours or days after the crash, seek medical care immediately. Consider seeing a doctor within 24-72 hours of the accident as a precaution.

After the initial check-up, follow all medical advice and treatment plans diligently. Keep copies of all medical records, bills, and discharge papers. Not only does this help you recover fully, but it also provides a clear record linking your injuries to the accident – which will be vital if you pursue compensation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I need to call the police for a minor fender-bender in Arizona? A: AArizona law requires you to notify law enforcement if a crash results in injury or death. While police are not legally required to respond to every minor property-damage-only accident, it’s still a good idea to call or file a report whenever possible. An official report can be very helpful later if injuries appear or there is a dispute with insurance. Never leave the scene without exchanging required information.

Q: Should I see a doctor even if I feel fine after the accident? A: Yes, absolutely. After an accident your adrenaline is high, which can mask pain and injury symptoms. Injuries like whiplash, soft tissue strains, concussions, or internal injuries may not show obvious signs right away. Medical professionals advise getting a check-up as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours of the accident.

Q: Can I still recover damages if I was partially at fault for the car accident? A: Yes. Arizona follows a “pure comparative negligence” rule. This means each party can still recover compensation even if they were partly to blame, but their recovery is reduced by their percentage of fault. For example, if you were 20% at fault and the other driver 80% at fault, you could recover 80% of your damages.

Q: How long do I have to file a claim or lawsuit after a car accident in Arizona? A: In Arizona, the statute of limitations for filing a personal injury lawsuit from a car accident is generally two years from the date of the accident. If you miss this two-year window, yyou will usually lose your right to file a lawsuit seeking compensation, with only very limited exceptions. It’s best not to delay – important evidence can disappear and memories can fade over time.
